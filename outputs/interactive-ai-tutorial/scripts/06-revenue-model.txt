The third example is a revenue model, and here the priority is transparency. A slider is only useful if we understand what changes when we move it. We will use sample business assumptions and simple arithmetic, then check the results ourselves. This is a teaching model, not a forecast of a real business, and we are not giving the numbers a confidence score they have not earned.

Start with twenty thousand monthly visitors, a two percent conversion rate, and an average order value of two hundred and fifty dirhams. Two percent of twenty thousand gives four hundred orders. Multiply four hundred by two hundred and fifty, and the revenue is one hundred thousand dirhams. Those are the first two checks we can perform without trusting the interface.

Now add a gross margin of sixty percent and fixed monthly costs of thirty thousand dirhams. Sixty percent of one hundred thousand is sixty thousand, and subtracting the fixed costs leaves thirty thousand. The label on screen describes this as contribution after fixed costs. It is not a complete accounting statement, because the simplified model may leave out taxes, financing, returns, and other items that matter to an actual business.

The benefit of the interface is that we can change an assumption and immediately see the mechanical effect. Increase the conversion rate from two percent to three percent while keeping everything else the same. Orders become six hundred, revenue becomes one hundred and fifty thousand, and the result after the costs in this model becomes sixty thousand. That does not prove we can achieve a three percent conversion rate. It shows what the formula would produce if that assumption were true.

Now reduce conversion to one percent. Orders fall to two hundred, revenue falls to fifty thousand, and sixty percent of that revenue exactly covers the thirty thousand in fixed costs. This gives us a useful check: under these particular assumptions, one percent is the break-even conversion rate for the costs included here. Change the costs or margin and that break-even point will change too.

Notice how different this is from repeatedly asking a chat to calculate another scenario. Once the formula is in a working tool, we can explore it directly. The AI was useful for helping construct the interface, but each slider movement is just a calculation in the browser. That makes the result easy to inspect, repeat, and explain to somebody else.

Before using a model like this in a meeting, write down where each assumption came from. A real visitor count, a historical conversion rate, and a guess about future order value should not be presented as equally certain. The interface can make arithmetic faster, but the evidence behind the inputs still determines whether the discussion is useful.
