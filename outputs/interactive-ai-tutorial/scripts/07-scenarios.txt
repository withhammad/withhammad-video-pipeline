Let’s look at the scenario table rather than a single headline number. In this example, the conservative scenario reduces the conversion assumption by twenty-five percent, the base case leaves it unchanged, and the stretch scenario increases it by twenty-five percent. We are changing only conversion. The other inputs stay fixed so that we can see exactly where the difference comes from.

With a base conversion rate of two percent, those scenarios become one and a half percent, two percent, and two and a half percent. That is a relative change of twenty-five percent, not an increase or decrease of twenty-five percentage points. The distinction matters, because those two phrases describe very different calculations. A good interface should make this assumption visible rather than hiding it behind attractive scenario names.

Using our sample visitor count and order value, the revenue numbers become seventy-five thousand, one hundred thousand, and one hundred and twenty-five thousand dirhams. After applying the same margin and fixed costs, the corresponding results are fifteen thousand, thirty thousand, and forty-five thousand. Take a moment to compare the table with those calculations. We want the numbers to be boringly consistent.

Next, change the fixed costs while leaving the sales assumptions alone. Revenue should remain unchanged, because fixed costs do not enter the revenue formula. The amount left after costs should change by exactly the amount we added or removed. This is another valuable test because it checks the relationship between controls, not just one output value.

You can do the same with gross margin. Moving the margin slider should affect the result after costs, but it should not change the number of orders. If everything on a dashboard moves whenever any slider changes, that may be a sign that the model is not separating its variables properly. Understanding these relationships makes you a better reviewer of generated tools.

The export button turns the scenario table into a spreadsheet-friendly file. Open that file and compare its rows with the screen before sharing it. Include the assumptions in the surrounding discussion, because a table without context can easily be mistaken for a forecast. You may also want the tool to export a timestamp and the input values so a teammate can reproduce the calculation later.

One improvement I would request next is sensitivity analysis: show which assumption has the largest effect within a clearly defined range. But I would not ask the AI to invent a business stability score without defining what the score means and how it is validated. More numbers do not automatically create more insight. A small model with visible assumptions is often more useful than a complicated dashboard whose reasoning nobody can explain.
